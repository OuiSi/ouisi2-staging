OuiSi · Math

Ordered pairs

Beta
Grades 4–5 Teaching 35 min Plus 4 to set out, 3 to clear Class groups of 4–6 Set any Prep 25 cards per group, labels
set out 4′   Both axes are numbers 7′ The split 8′ Hold the convention 12′ Where have you seen this 5′ Close 3′   clear 3′
Goal
Half the room goes to the wrong square, and only then does the rule about which number comes first get a reason to exist.
Materials
One OuiSi Photo-Card set. Twenty-five cards per group in a five-by-five square. Labels for both axes.
Grade 4
Numbers on both axes, counting from the top-left square, the convention named after the mistake. Stop after step three — about 27 min.
Grade 5
Count up from the bottom left instead, then connect it to the coordinate plane they will meet in algebra. Full 35 min.

1 · Both axes are numbers

7 min

Groups lay twenty-five cards five by five. Label across with 1 to 5, and down with 1 to 5 as well.

Both ways are numbers now. No letters to tell them apart.

Say nothing yet about which is read first. The ambiguity is deliberate and the lesson depends on it surviving the next few minutes.

2 · The split

8 min

Everybody put a finger on two, four.

Wait. Look around the room. Say nothing while they notice.

Half the room will be on one card and half on another. Let that sit for a few seconds — the silence is doing the work.

You are in two different places. Why?

So which number do we read first? And who decides?

Then give the convention, after they have felt the need for one.

Across first, then down. That is our rule from now on.

It is not a law of nature. It is an agreement — and that is exactly why we all have to use the same one.

Resist saying everyone does it this way, because they do not. A spreadsheet names the column first, a map reference gives the letter first, and some grids count rows before columns. The lesson is stronger for being true: the order is chosen, and a grid is useless until you know which choice it made.

3 · Hold the convention

12 min

One child turns away. The group picks a card silently.

Describe your card. The caller gives the ordered pair.

Then make it harder.

Caller, now give us a pair and we will all point. If you say it backward, we all go to the wrong place together.

Having to produce a correct pair under pressure is where the convention sticks. Expect reversals and treat them as ordinary rather than as errors.

4 · Where have you seen this

5 min

Where else do two numbers tell you one place?

Maps, seats in a theater, spreadsheets, screens, latitude and longitude. For grade 5, this is the moment to say the word coordinates and draw the axes on the board over the top of the card grid.

5 · Close

3 min

Give me the pair for the card you would keep, and say it the right way round.

Watch for

  • Telling them the rule in step one. The whole lesson is the split in step two. Give the convention early and you have taught a fact instead of a reason.
  • Treating reversals as carelessness. They are not. The convention is arbitrary and holding an arbitrary rule under pressure is the skill being built.
  • Calling the corner the origin. It is not one. This grid is numbered 1 to 5 and counts down from the top; a coordinate plane starts at zero and counts up from the bottom. Say corner here, keep origin for grade 5 when the axes go on the board, and name the difference out loud rather than letting it surface later.
  • Crooked grids. Straighten before step two, since ambiguity about the grid muddies the ambiguity you want.

Adapt

  • Grade 4 — stop after step three. The convention and holding it is plenty.
  • Grade 5 — flip the vertical axis so it counts up from the bottom, and draw the two axes over the grid. The bridge to algebra is worth the five minutes.
  • Battleship version — two groups, two grids, cards hidden, called by ordered pair. Works as a follow-on lesson.
  • Multilingual learners — numbers carry everything. The only language load is across and down.
  • Motor or vision needs — wider spacing, and a partner may point on request.
  • Extension — give three pairs that make a straight line, and ask for a fourth that continues it.
Curriculum links. Locating a point from two numerical coordinates · Treating order as an agreement rather than a fact · Holding a convention consistently across a group · Recognizing coordinate notation elsewhere.

What's OuiSi?

Pronounced "We-See," OuiSi translates to "Yes Yes" from other languages. It's a nod to our belief that pictures are a language nearly all of us speak. OuiSi is based in the NY Hudson Valley.
