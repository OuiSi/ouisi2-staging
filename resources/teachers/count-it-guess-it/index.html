OuiSi · Math

Count it, guess it

Beta
Grades 3–5 Teaching 35 min Plus 3 to set out, 3 to clear Class any size Set any Prep board for the estimates
set out 3′   Too many to count 10′ Make the method explicit 10′ Break your own method 10′ Close 5′   clear 3′
Goal
Children meet a picture where counting one by one is hopeless, invent a way to estimate it, and learn that the method is what gets judged — because nobody in the room is going to check the answer.
Materials
One OuiSi Photo-Card set. A few crowded cards — scattered stones, leaves, a flock, a texture. Board for the estimates.
Grade 3
Cover half and double it — about 25 min
Grade 4
Sample a corner and scale up — the full 35 min
Grade 5
Say what makes your sample unreliable, and give a range instead of a number — the full 35 min

1 · Too many to count

10 min

Hand each group a card where exact counting is hopeless. Do not warn them. Keep one crowded card back for yourself — step two needs every group looking at the same picture.

How many? Write a number down.

Give them long enough to try counting and fail. Then intervene once.

Now make a better guess. What could you do to the picture to help?

Someone will cover half and count the rest, or count a corner and multiply. That move is estimation inventing itself, and it is worth stopping the whole room to show.

2 · Make the method explicit

10 min

Hold up the card you kept back, where the whole room can see it. Every group estimates this one, and you write the spread on the board.

They have to be estimating the same picture for the spread to mean anything. Comparing numbers from six different cards tells you nothing at all.

Look at the range. Who is closest? We genuinely do not know.

Tell us how you got your number. Not the number — the method.

Rewarding method over answer is the whole point, and it works because nobody here is going to sit and count them. A well-reasoned wrong number beats a lucky right one, and here you can say that honestly.

3 · Break your own method

10 min

Your method assumed something. What was it?

If you counted a corner and multiplied, you assumed the rest of the picture is as crowded as that corner. On an uneven photograph that is badly wrong, and children can see it is wrong by looking.

Then the grade 5 move.

Give me a range instead of a number. Smallest it could sensibly be, largest it could sensibly be.

Now find a card where your method would work badly. Why that one?

4 · Close

5 min

When do grown-ups estimate instead of counting? Give me a real one.

Crowds, crops, time, money, distance. Take three, then stop.

Watch for

  • Wanting the real answer. Nobody in the room has it, you included. There is a true number on that card and no practical way to reach it today, which is a different and more honest thing to say than there is no answer. Say it at the start — some children find not knowing genuinely uncomfortable and need it named.
  • A million. That is a refusal, not an estimate. Ask what they could do to the picture to get closer, which is step one's real question.
  • Doggedly counting 200 stones. Admire the persistence, then ask how long the whole deck would take.
  • Methods left implicit. A group that cannot state its method has not estimated, it has guessed. Step two is where that gets caught.

Adapt

  • Grade 3 — stop after step two. Cover half and double is a complete lesson.
  • Grade 5 — ranges throughout, and compare whose range was narrow and still sensible.
  • Number anxiety — nothing here is marked right or wrong, so a child who freezes on exact arithmetic can take part fully. An estimate can still be unreasonable, and the way to say so is to ask about the method rather than the number.
  • Children who think faster than they write — let them explain the method aloud while a partner records it.
  • Multilingual learners — the method can be shown by pointing at the part of the card they used.
  • Science tie-in — the same sampling logic as counting a population from a quadrat.

What's OuiSi?

Pronounced "We-See," OuiSi translates to "Yes Yes" from other languages. It's a nod to our belief that pictures are a language nearly all of us speak. OuiSi is based in the NY Hudson Valley.
