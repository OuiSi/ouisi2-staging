OuiSi · Visual arts

Connecting colors

Beta
Grades K–3 Teaching 35 min Plus 2 to set out, 3 to clear Class any size Set any — OuiSi Original is ideal Prep none
set out 2′   Everyone looks 5′ Chains, in fours 15′ Make it harder 10′ Close 5′   clear 3′
Goal
Children find and defend color relationships, and look closely at what actually happens where two colors meet, by seeing it rather than being told.
Materials
One OuiSi Photo-Card set. Optional paper and color materials for the extension.
K–1
Steps 1 and 2 as far as the pairs, then close. Two cards each, four to a pair, no harder prompts — about 16 min teaching.
Grades 2–3
Everything, including the fours and all three harder prompts — the full 35 min.

1 · Everyone looks

5 min

Spread cards face up, six per child, so nobody reaches across the room. Do not sort them and do not hand them out yet. Six each leaves a reserve in a class of thirty, which step 2 needs for swaps.

Look for thirty seconds. Do not touch anything.

Now point — do not say it — to a color you can find in three different cards.

Everyone points at once. No hands up, no turns, nobody waits.

2 · Chains, in fours

15 min

Alone, two minutes — every child takes exactly two cards.

Take two cards that share a color.

Pairs, four minutes — two children, four cards between them.

Lay all four in a row, each sharing a color with its neighbor. Say each link out loud as you place it.

If your two pairs will not join, swap a card for one from the table, or keep two shorter rows.

The new link between the two children's pairs is the work — but two reds and two blues may genuinely not meet, and the escape is there so nobody is stuck proving the impossible.

K–1 stops here, after the pairs, and goes straight to the close.

Fours, six minutes — two pairs join, eight cards, one row.

Make one row from all eight. Some links will not survive — rebuild until every card shares a color with its neighbor.

If two of you see different links, leave both on the table until you agree which one to use.

All, three minutes

Name one link your group disagreed about. Just the link — do not tell me who won.

3 · Make it harder

10 min

Same eight cards. Three prompts, given one at a time.

Find a connection using a color you can barely see.

Find two cards where the same color feels different.

Warm against cool, light against dark. Children describe it before they have the words, so supply them after.

Find a place where two colors meet. What happens at the join — do they blend, overlap, or stay separate?

Say meet, not mix. Describe the boundary you can see — a gradient, an overlap, or a hard edge — and name which. The photograph alone will not tell you how those colors were made.

4 · Close

5 min

Show me a link you almost did not make.

One child, one pair of cards. Do not go around the room.

Watch for

  • There are no matches. Do not explain — model. Place two cards yourself and say the connection out loud. Do it twice. Watching is what makes it land, not telling.
  • Sorting by object — all the animals together, instead of by color. Redirect to what they can see: I do not need to know what it is. What does it look like?
  • Children who like rules can find open play pointless. Give them a target they can measure: the longest row where no two links use the same color.

Adapt

  • Color vision — some children see color differently, and this lesson rests on it. Say at the start that a link may use lightness, pattern or texture instead of color. Never correct a child about what they see.
  • Multilingual learners — accept the color word in any language, then supply the English. The card carries the meaning.
  • Literal thinkers and autistic children — literal color matches are correct. Name them as strong, and do not push for abstraction.
  • Attention and ADHD — cards stay on the table, children stand, and rounds stay short.
  • Anxiety — stop at pairs. No whole-class share, no timer, and no winner.
  • Motor needs — cards slide rather than lift, and a partner may place on request.
Curriculum links. Identifying and naming colors · Describing what happens where two colors meet · Classifying by a visual attribute · Justifying a choice out loud · Collaborative problem solving.

What's OuiSi?

Pronounced "We-See," OuiSi translates to "Yes Yes" from other languages. It's a nod to our belief that pictures are a language nearly all of us speak. OuiSi is based in the NY Hudson Valley.
