What happened first

The whole guide, to read before the lesson.

Goal
Children build an order that carries a change, tell it using sequence words, and then hear it told back by someone who was not in their head. The telling back is the part that teaches.
Time
Eight minutes is the floor: three cards, one telling, one retell, with you supplying the words if they stall. The dial is the number of cards. Three is a kindergarten order and about eight to ten minutes; six is grade 3 and about eighteen, because every extra card adds an argument about where it goes. Running it twice with three cards beats running it once with six.
Materials
One OuiSi Photo-Card set, three to six cards per pair. The words first, then and last written up where they stay visible.
K
Three cards, and you supply the words the first time round — about 8 min
Grade 1
Three or four cards, and they use the words themselves — about 10 min
Grades 2–3
Six cards, and the retell has to come back in the right order — about 18 min

1 · Three cards, one order

5 min

Pairs, three cards each pair, face up.

Put these three in an order where something changes.

To a pair who have just arranged them by colour.

What changes from the first one to the last one? If nothing changes, swap a card.

That one phrase — an order where something changes — is doing all the work. Put them in order gets you prettiest-first. The change is what makes it a sequence instead of a row.

2 · Tell it with the words

8 min

Now tell it. Start with first.

First ______. Then ______. Last ______.

Point at the three words on the board rather than saying them.

Pointing instead of prompting is the difference between the child producing the word and you producing it. Wait a full five seconds before you help — most children arrive at three, and the ones who need longer need the silence more, not less.

3 · Someone else tells it back

5 min

Two pairs join up at one table. The first pair tells their sequence, pointing at their own cards as they go.

Tell them your order. First, then, last.

Then the listening pair tells it back from the same cards.

Now you tell it back. You two, you can't help.

Did it come back in the right order? Which card did you go to first?

They hear the account before they retell it, which is what makes this a retell rather than a guess at someone else's cards. If it comes back out of order, two different things are worth separating: whether the telling was clear, and whether the cards carried the change. Asking which card they went to first usually tells you which. Swap roles if you have the minutes.

Watch for

  • Orders that are just cards they like. Ask what changes. It is the only correction this lesson needs.
  • Describing instead of sequencing. A dog. A tree. A boat. is three descriptions in a row, not a sequence.
  • Supplying the sequence words. If you say then, they will not.
  • A pair who disagree about the order. Let them lay out both and tell both. Two defensible orders from the same three cards is the best thing that can happen here.
  • Six cards in kindergarten. It turns into sorting and the telling never happens.

Adapt

  • Speech and language — this is a core expressive-language task and it scales cleanly by card count. For articulation work, choose cards whose names carry the target sound and the sequence words come free.
  • Multilingual learners — take the order told in a home language first, then ask for it again in English with the three words on the board.
  • Kindergarten — you tell the first order yourself, then they tell the next one. Modelling once is worth more than explaining twice.
  • Want it harder — six cards, and one of the middle cards has to be removable without breaking the story. They have to work out which one is load-bearing.
  • Writing — the told order becomes three written sentences. It is the shortest real paragraph in the library.

Adapted from speech and language practice. Arranging picture cards and then retelling the sequence is long-standing practice in speech and language therapy, where the retell is the measure rather than the arrangement. The arrangement is the easy half, and it is the half most classroom sequencing activities stop at.

What's OuiSi?

Pronounced "We-See," OuiSi translates to "Yes Yes" from other languages. It's a nod to our belief that pictures are a language nearly all of us speak. OuiSi is based in the NY Hudson Valley.
