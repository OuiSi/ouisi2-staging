OuiSi · Thinking skills

Say what you see first

Beta
Grades 2–5 Teaching 12–25 min Plus 3 to set out, 2 to clear Class groups of 4–6 Set any Prep one question on the board, 20+ cards spread out
3 min to set out   The question, and a silent choice 3 min Describe, don't explain 10 min Now everyone else reads it 7 min Then say why you chose it 5 min   2 min to clear

The whole guide, to read before the lesson.

Goal
Children learn to say what is actually in front of them before they say what they think it means — and to hear other people's readings of their own choice before they defend it.
Time
Twelve minutes is the floor: one child presents, one round of readings, one reason. The dial is how many children present. Each one costs about four minutes, so three or four presenters fills the twenty-five, and the whole class is two lessons rather than one long one. Running one presenter well beats running eight in a hurry.
Materials
One OuiSi Photo-Card set, twenty or more cards spread face up where children can walk round them. One question, written up before they arrive.
Grades 2–3
A concrete question. One presenter, then one more if it is going well — about 12 min
Grades 4–5
An abstract question, and three or four presenters — the full 25 min

1 · The question, and a silent choice

3 min

The question is already on the board when they come in. Read it out once and do not explain it.

Here is the question. Don't answer it yet.

Walk round and take the card that answers it for you. Don't tell anybody which one you took.

Questions that work: What is hard about being new? · What does our class do well? · What is this unit really about? · Which card is this character, right now? A question with a correct answer kills the lesson — there has to be room for twenty different cards to be right.

2 · Describe, don't explain

10 min

One child holds their card up. They say only what is in it.

Tell us only what is in the picture. No reasons yet. Just what is there.

They will try to explain within about six words. Stop them, warmly, every time.

That's why you picked it. I want what's in it. What colour, what's at the edges, what's happening.

This is the hard part and the whole lesson. Separating description from interpretation is the move that transfers — to a text, a graph, a science result, an argument in the yard. Children have almost never been asked to do it on purpose.

3 · Now everyone else reads it

7 min

The rest of you. Looking at that card, what do you think it says about the question?

Take three or four readings. The child holding the card does not speak and does not nod.

Someone will read it in a way the chooser never thought of. That is the payoff, and it is why they are not allowed to explain first — a reason given up front shuts every other reading down before it arrives.

4 · Then say why you chose it

5 min

Now tell us why you chose it. And did anybody see something in it you hadn't?

The second half of that question is the one worth waiting for. Expect I didn't see the bit at the back — on their own card, that they chose. Say nothing more; it lands by itself.

Watch for

  • Letting the chooser explain early. One sentence of reasoning in step 2 and step 3 collapses into agreeing with them.
  • The class guessing what the chooser meant. Redirect: Not what she meant. What you see.
  • Abstract questions too young. In grade 2, what does our class do well works and what is fairness does not.
  • Rushing to get everyone in. Four presenters done properly teaches the move. Twenty done fast teaches that it is a sharing circle.
  • A child who wants a card someone has taken. Two children on one card is fine and often better — they describe it differently.

Adapt

  • Multilingual learners — step 2 is pure concrete vocabulary, which is the easiest step in the library to do in a second language. Put the words they use on the board.
  • Anxiety — presenting is the exposed seat. Let a pair present one card together, or let a child describe while you hold it up.
  • Tie it to a text — the question becomes which card is this character, and step 3 turns into a discussion of evidence in the book.
  • Staff meetings and parent evenings — this runs unchanged with adults, and the question becomes the one you actually need answered.
  • Want it harder — ban the words that give the game away. No sad, no happy, no busy in step 2. Only what is physically there.

Adapted from facilitation practice. The shape — choose an image for a framing question, describe before interpreting, hear others before you explain — comes from image-based dialogue methods used in leadership and team facilitation. It was built for adults and it works almost unchanged from about grade 2.

What's OuiSi?

Pronounced "We-See," OuiSi translates to "Yes Yes" from other languages. It's a nod to our belief that pictures are a language nearly all of us speak. OuiSi is based in the NY Hudson Valley.
